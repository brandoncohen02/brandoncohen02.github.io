AI & Employment

How does Artificial Intelligence affect Employment? Employment outlook, public perception, and government response.

Brandon Cohen

Introduction

The growth of AI is reshaping the global labor force at an unpredictable pace. As machines advance and become more capable of solving complex problems, the need for humans to perform tasks is looming. Some view AI as a threat to job security and economic stability. These reactions have sparked intense public concern, with more people concerned about losing their current jobs or others missing out on the opportunity to work in the future. However, this fear might be disproportionate to the actual data. With regulations, it may protect workers and ensure a seamless transition to the future of work with artificial intelligence.

This multimodal composition will explore AI's current and projected impact on employment, analyze the role of fear in shaping public belief, and look into the government's responsibility in addressing these issues. While some believe that individuals in the white-collar sector may be replaced, others argue that automation will be utilized to enhance job performance. AI continues to grow, and governments are facing increasing pressure regarding whether they should be responsible for regulating it.

Employment Outlook

The rise of artificial intelligence (AI) has had a tremendous impact on the labor market across the world, especially among white-collar careers. Recently, major companies such as Microsoft, Amazon, and Salesforce, among others, have made headlines by announcing layoffs as AI becomes more advanced and capable of doing unimagined work. Tasks that were once handled by humans are now being automated by AI. Nearly all companies are increasing their investment in AI, with some office jobs starting to disappear.

Researcher and renowned professor Eric Dahlin, who lectures at Brigham Young University, conducted a study on this topic: "Findings from my analysis indicate that AI-induced job displacement occurs, on average, for respondents with white-collar jobs" (Dahlin, 2024, p. 2). Dahlin's research suggests that office jobs are at a greater risk of being replaced by AI than other employment types. This shows that automation can threaten any job, regardless of the skill level; even educated workers are at risk. Dahlin's research found that those belonging to disadvantaged groups or those who faced systematic disadvantages in the labor force have stronger feelings of vulnerability than others.

The study also notes that "The five most highly exposed jobs are mechanical drafters (those who prepare diagrams of mechanical devices), billing and postal clerks, biological technicians, bookkeeping and auditing clerks, and industrial designers" (Dahlin, 2024, p. 2). In this context, this exposure means the chance of a job being switched to automation and artificial intelligence. These jobs all require cognitive tasks, which AI can do very well. AI experts predict that AI will not be able to replace creativity or critical thinking skills, which are highly sought after in the labor market.

Despite this, many jobs are at risk of being replaced by artificial intelligence. Mohamad Hassan Soueidan and Rodwan Shoghari are both associated with Al-Jinan University in Lebanon and have projected long-term that "A 2013 study by Frey and Osborne suggested that as many as 47% of U.S. jobs could be at risk due to automation over the next few decades" (Soueidan & Shoghari, 2024, p. 208). As of January 2025, there are currently 170.1 million civilians in the labor force. If 47% of those jobs are replaced, that translates to approximately 80 million workers, which could adversely affect the economy with significant unemployment rates. Both quotes highlight the widespread impact of AI on employment. Millions of jobs are expected to be replaced by AI automation, regardless of whether the job requires high skills. Artificial intelligence is advancing, and it can now perform complex tasks once thought impossible.

Artificial Intelligence Anxiety in the Workplace

While artificial intelligence has revolutionized our lives, it has also led to a widespread feeling of anxiety and fear due to job insecurity. During a recent conversation with my friend, a computer major here at Rutgers, he expressed his worries about the future of AI. He's anxious that his degree will lose value if AI can do the work for a cheaper price. He expressed that with computer science, AI can write and debug code. This concern reflects what many feel in this new AI-driven world, where many jobs may become obsolete.

The fear of AI replacing jobs is not solely based on personal experience but is also significantly influenced by public perception. Eric Dahlin concludes, "These results reveal a significant gap between the fear of job loss because of AI and whether a respondent lost a job to AI" (Dahlin, 2024, p. 8). This indicates that public anxiety may be disproportionate to job loss, suggesting that fear alone can shape public opinion, even if not many jobs are lost. These results can foster an environment that strays away from AI and other new technologies, as it is seen psychologically as a job destroyer.

Many experts predict that we must prepare for a world increasingly driven by AI. Giselle Rampersad, who is the Dean of Education at the College of Science and Engineering at Flinders University, argues, "Rather than fearing the robot, attention is needed on equipping the workforce with innovation skills needed for the future of work" (Rampersad, 2020, p. 73). The approach indicated here drifts away from the helplessness narrative to being action-driven. It suggests that AI is inevitable, and we should embrace it as an opportunity to learn new skills and adapt alongside technology. Employers can host training workshops to familiarize their employees with artificial intelligence. Public fear is a common reaction to rapid change; connecting perception to reality allows a society to build resilience. About 66% of American workers feel some anxiety about AI taking their jobs in the future.

The Role of Government in Regulating AI

As artificial intelligence continues to reshape the job market, it is apparent that some form of government action is imperative. While innovation and upskilling in the private sector are part of the solution, these initiatives alone are insufficient to address the widespread impacts of AI. These solutions are not enough to handle the job displacements nationwide. Soueidan and Shoghari believe that "governments should not just react to job losses once they have occurred but anticipate these changes and implement policies to minimize negative impacts and maximize potential benefits" (Soueidan & Shoghari, 2024, p. 221). Having a proactive government that takes action to ensure its workers are protected and sufficiently prepared for the future of AI.

Since AI is a relatively new technology, the government must act ahead, passing legislation and funding retraining programs to support this. Soueidan and Shoghari argue that policymakers should pass, "Re-skilling and up-skilling tasks are pivotal to make certain that people displaced with the aid of AI can transition into new or converted roles" (Soueidan & Shoghari, 2024, p. 219). The upskilling and reskilling program prepares workers for a changing labor market. The government should equip its workforce with the tools necessary to stay on top of the ever-changing job market. Investments in the labor market help reduce the unemployment rate and improve living quality.

Another perspective of this comes from Giselle Rampersad, who claims, "However, reaping the benefits of Industry 4.0 is not just a technological challenge but also a human issue, requiring attention being placed on upskilling and also the human dimensions of major change" (Rampersad, 2020, p. 68). This perspective indicates that adapting to AI is more than adjusting your skillset. It requires addressing the emotional and social needs of the workers during this period. As for government policy, it should consider doing it both practically and humanely. Together, these quotes emphasize that while anticipating job displacement is important, training workers to ensure they are emotionally and socially prepared for the transition is equally crucial.

Concluding Thoughts

Artificial Intelligence is rapidly reshaping the workforce today, displacing some jobs while creating demand for new skills in emerging fields. Public perception is making AI seem much worse than it is. People worldwide fear losing their jobs, although data shows otherwise. As AI becomes more widespread, governments should be proactive and implement solutions, such as upskilling or reskilling programs, to better prepare the workforce for the AI change. There is no way of delaying the growth and advancement of AI, but we should try to embrace it. As we have seen with computers, people were worried about these machines taking over their jobs, but they actually created jobs. In my opinion, the best thing we can do is stay flexible, learn new skills, and push for strong government policies that support workers. AI doesn't have to be something we fear; it could be something we use to build a better and brighter future.
